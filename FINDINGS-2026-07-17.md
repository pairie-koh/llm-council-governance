# Phase 2 Findings: LLM Councils on Humanity's Last Exam

2026-07-17. For Andy Hall and Rohit Krishnan. Repo `pairie-koh/llm-council-governance`, branch `frontier-rerun` (merged to `main`). Every number below reproduces offline from committed JSON with the three commands in the appendix; no number is quoted from memory.

---

## How to read this document

Phase 2 is not one experiment. It is five, and they answer different questions. Earlier drafts blended them; this version separates them so each result stands on its own. Each experiment changes exactly one thing and holds the rest fixed:

| # | Experiment | What it varies | What it holds fixed | The question it answers |
|---|------------|----------------|---------------------|--------------------------|
| 0 | Solo baselines | nothing (independent answers) | — | How good is each model alone, and how correlated are their errors? |
| 1 | Structure comparison | the aggregation mechanism | the 4 answers, Fable as chair | Which council structure produces the best answer? |
| 2 | Chairman strength | who chairs (Fable vs Opus) | the mechanism (cabinet) | Does deliberation help or hurt, and does that depend on the decider? |
| 3 | Recognition vs generation | (offline re-cut) | — | Why do 1 and 2 come out the way they do? |
| 4 | Query-type sweep | (offline re-cut by category) | — | Where do councils help, and where is the disagreement signal trustworthy? |

**The paired design.** Experiments 1, 2, and 3 all re-aggregate the *same frozen stage-1 answers* from Experiment 0. Every comparison is therefore within-question: identical questions, identical member answers, only the aggregation rule or the chair changes. This is what lets small accuracy differences mean something. Unanimous questions are decided identically by every structure, so only the 130 disagreement questions discriminate between methods; that is the working set for Experiments 1-3.

## Experiment 0: Solo baselines (the foundation)

### Setup

Council v2 is the strongest model per vendor as of July 2026:

| Role | Model |
|------|-------|
| Member + chairman | Claude Fable 5 (`anthropic/claude-fable-5`) |
| Member | GPT-5.6 Sol (`openai/gpt-5.6-sol`) |
| Member | Gemini 3.1 Pro (`google/gemini-3.1-pro-preview`) |
| Member | Grok 4.5 (`x-ai/grok-4.5`) |
| Side-arm (not a council member) | Claude Opus 4.8 (`anthropic/claude-opus-4.8`) |

Benchmark: Humanity's Last Exam, text-only multiple choice. Biology/Medicine and Chemistry were excluded before any council ran, because the scout showed Fable's safety classifiers refuse 67-71% of questions in those two categories versus 0-11% elsewhere, and Fable chairs. That leaves a **374-question eligible pool**. Each model answered every question once, independently, with no visibility of the others. Refusals and non-answers are recorded as their own outcome and count against accuracy; they are never retried on a fallback model.

### Result: one model dominates

| Model | Accuracy (n=374) | Notes |
|-------|------------------|-------|
| Claude Fable 5 | **60.4%** | 13 refusals + 21 no_answer counted against |
| GPT-5.6 Sol | 48.1% | |
| Claude Opus 4.8 (side-arm) | 46.8% | |
| Grok 4.5 | 45.2% | |
| Gemini 3.1 Pro | 43.0% | 70 no_answer counted against |

Fable leads the field by 12 points. That gap is the single most important fact in phase 2; every later result follows from it.

### Result: the questions split into three regimes

Of the 374 questions, 284 are "clean" (all four members produced a scoreable letter). Those partition into:

- **154 unanimous** (all four agree). 103 are correct: a unanimous council is right **66.9%** of the time.
- **130 disagreement** (members split). This is the working set for Experiments 1-3.
- Within the 130, **35 are minority-correct** (exactly one member right). Fable is that lone-correct member on **21 of 35**; the other three combined account for 14.

On the disagreement subset, the dominance widens:

| Model | Accuracy on the 130 disagreement questions |
|-------|--------------------------------------------|
| Fable 5 | **66.2%** (86/130) |
| Gemini 3.1 Pro | 38.5% |
| GPT-5.6 | 33.8% |
| Grok 4.5 | 27.7% |
| Opus 4.8 (side-arm) | 41.1% (53/129 clean) |

**Solo Fable on these 130 questions, 66.2%, is the baseline every council structure has to beat.**

### Result: errors are correlated, so voting cannot average them out

Pairwise phi on correct/wrong outcomes across the full 374-question pool, plus the rate at which two wrong models pick the *same* wrong letter:

| Pair | phi | Same wrong letter when both wrong |
|------|-----|-----------------------------------|
| Fable x Opus (intra-vendor) | +0.63 | 91/104 (88%) |
| Fable x Gemini | +0.64 | 70/87 (80%) |
| Fable x Grok | +0.61 | 88/106 (83%) |
| Fable x GPT | +0.58 | 75/101 (74%) |
| Opus x Gemini | +0.63 | 93/114 (82%) |
| Opus x Grok | +0.62 | 120/146 (82%) |
| Gemini x Grok | +0.63 | 93/121 (77%) |
| Gemini x GPT | +0.60 | 88/115 (77%) |
| GPT x Grok | +0.56 | 108/143 (76%) |
| Opus x GPT | +0.53 | 101/134 (75%) |

### What Experiment 0 establishes

The Condorcet jury theorem, which underwrites all vote-based aggregation, requires errors to be independent. They are not. Every pair here sits at phi +0.53 to +0.64, and when two models are both wrong they choose the same wrong answer three-quarters of the time or more. Under those conditions a vote does not cancel noise; it concentrates shared error into a confident majority. Combined with the 12-point dominance of one member, this is the setup that makes every downstream aggregation result negative.

## Experiment 1: Which aggregation structure is best?

### Setup

Hold the inputs fixed (the four stage-1 answers) and, where a chair exists, hold the chair fixed (Fable). Vary only the mechanism that turns four answers into one. Score on the 130 disagreement questions against the solo-Fable baseline of 66.2%.

| Structure | Mechanism |
|-----------|-----------|
| jury | Offline plurality vote over the four stage-1 letters. Ties recorded as ties, counted incorrect. Sees letters only. |
| cabinet | One Fable chairman call: reads the four answers plus reasoning excerpts, anonymized as Councilor A-D (per-question seeded shuffle), picks a letter. |
| court | One advocate brief per distinct proposed letter (advocates are the non-Anthropic members, round-robin, so no Fable advocate argues before the Fable judge); Fable judges from the briefs. |
| peer_review | Each member anonymously ranks all four answers (its own included, unmarked); Borda count decides. Unparseable ballots dropped; cross-letter Borda ties counted incorrect. |

### Result

| Structure | Disagreement accuracy (n=130) | Rescued (of 35 lone-correct) | Ties / no_answer | New OpenRouter cost |
|-----------|-------------------------------|------------------------------|------------------|---------------------|
| **solo Fable (baseline)** | **66.2% (86/130)** | 21/35 by definition | 0 | $0 |
| court | 60.0% (78/130) | 16/35 | 3 no_answer | $8.31 |
| cabinet | 59.2% (77/130) | 16/35 | 0 | $0 |
| peer_review | 52.3% (68/130) | 5/35 | 4 ties | $24.19 |
| jury | 38.5% (50/130) | 0/35 | 25 ties | $0 |

### Analysis

**No structure beats the best member, and the ordering tracks how much member reasoning each mechanism actually consumes.**

- **Jury is destructive** (-27.7 points vs solo Fable). It sees only letters, so it has no way to tell a correlated-wrong majority from a correct one, and it rescued **0 of 35** lone-correct cells. That zero is forced by arithmetic: a single correct dissenter never holds a plurality. 25 of 130 cells (19%) were outright ties; even scoring ties as coin flips only lifts the jury to roughly 48%, still 18 points under the best member. This is Experiment 0's error correlation made concrete: shared wrong answers become confident majorities.
- **Peer review** sees reasoning but collapses it into rank ballots, which is vote-like; its Borda count buried lone-correct answers (5/35 rescues), landing it near the bottom.
- **Cabinet** reads the actual arguments and recovers most of the jury's loss (59.2%).
- **Court** forces one explicit written brief per candidate answer and lets a judge weigh them against each other. It is the best aggregation tested. Its 3 no_answers are counted against it; on the questions it did answer it is 78/127 = 61.4%.

Accepting the unanimous letter on the 154 unanimous questions and each structure's verdict on the 130 disagreement questions gives the pool-level accuracy over all 284 clean questions:

| Structure | Pool-level accuracy (n=284) |
|-----------|------------------------------|
| solo Fable | 66.5% (189/284) |
| court | 63.7% (181/284) |
| cabinet | 63.4% (180/284) |
| peer_review | 60.2% (171/284) |
| cabinet_opus | 59.9% (170/284) |
| jury | 53.9% (153/284) |

Adversarial advocacy is the right direction, and it still does not reach the best member.

### What is and is not statistically real

Using exact McNemar tests on the paired discordant counts (the honest test for this paired design):

- **jury vs solo Fable: decisive** (0 vs 36 discordant questions, p = 3e-11).
- **peer_review and cabinet_opus vs solo Fable: solid** (p = 0.0005 each).
- **cabinet vs solo Fable (p = 0.064) and court vs solo Fable (p = 0.115): directional only.**
- **court vs cabinet: not distinguishable** (78 vs 77 correct, discordant 10 vs 9, p = 1.0).

The endpoints of the ranking are real. The middle ordering (court vs cabinet vs peer_review) is not established at n=130. Cite "no structure beats the best member" and "jury is catastrophic"; do not cite "court beats cabinet."

## Experiment 2: Does the chairman's strength change deliberation's direction? (the herding result)

### Setup

This is a different cut from Experiment 1. There, we varied the structure and kept Fable in the chair. Here we hold the structure fixed (cabinet: read the four anonymized answers, pick one) and vary only who chairs. Fable is the strongest member; Opus 4.8 is weaker than the best member. Same 130 questions, same anonymized answer packets, same prompt. Only the model in the chair changes.

### Result

| Chairman | Chairman's solo score on the subset | As cabinet chairman | Change |
|----------|-------------------------------------|---------------------|--------|
| Fable 5 (strongest member) | 66.2% (86/130) | 59.2% (77/130) | **-7.0 points** |
| Opus 4.8 (weaker than best member) | 41.1% (53/129 clean) | 51.5% (67/130) | **+10.4 points** |

**The same council lifts a weak chairman and drags a strong one down.** Deliberation regresses the decider toward the council's blended quality: it moves accuracy from wherever it is concentrated toward wherever it is not.

### Analysis: rescue vs capitulation

Reading the council is not neutral exposure; it produces flips in both directions, and the net depends on which direction dominates.

- **Fable chair:** 14 questions where Fable's own stage-1 answer was right came out wrong after chairing, versus only 5 wrong-to-right. Net herding. The cabinet did rescue 16/35 lone-correct cells (something the jury cannot do), but paid for those rescues with more capitulations elsewhere. Individually, the Fable drop is directional (exact binomial p = 0.064).
- **Opus chair:** 21 questions Opus got wrong solo came out right after chairing, versus 7 the other way. Net updating. This lift is individually significant (p = 0.013).
- The chairman-identity contrast itself (Fable chair vs Opus chair, 19 vs 9 discordant) is p = 0.087: suggestive, not decisive, but the direction is unambiguous and the two per-chair effects point opposite ways.

### Worked examples

Anonymization makes the mechanism vivid, because the chairman cannot even recognize its own answer.

- **Chairman talks itself out of its own right answer** (`hle_66f8f56e204cc74a525a23b8`, Kenneth Burke's "Tribal No"). Fable's stage-1 answer E was the lone correct one. As chairman it saw its own answer relabeled "Councilor D" against three councilors on A, and ruled: *"Councilor D's argument, while sophisticated, misapplies Burke's idea/image distinction to the wrong negative. The majority reasoning better fits Burke's hortatory-first account... FINAL ANSWER: A."* It argued against itself and lost.
- **A lone wrong dissenter flips a correct majority** (`hle_6739691ecea30e65e1f96876`, reward range for geometric convergence of value iteration). Three members including Fable held the correct answer E; Grok alone argued A. The chairman found the lone dissenter's boundedness argument persuasive and ruled A, flipping off a correct 3-1 majority that included its own answer.

### What Experiment 2 establishes

Experiment 1 said the *mechanism* does not help. Experiment 2 says the *direction* of deliberation's effect is set by the chair's strength relative to the council: strong deciders herd downward, weak deciders update upward. This is the "updating vs herding" question with a directional answer, and it is orthogonal to the structure question.

## Experiment 3: Why? Recognition vs generation

### Setup

No new API calls. This re-cuts the cabinet and peer-review data to separate two distinct abilities:

- **Generation** = can the model produce the correct answer on its own (its stage-1 accuracy).
- **Recognition** = can the model pick the correct answer out of a slate of candidates when shown them.

A cross-review or ranking stage only adds value if recognition exceeds generation: a member that generated a wrong answer must be able to identify a peer's correct one. If recognition equals generation, ranking is redundant. This experiment measures the gap directly.

### Result: recognition beats generation for weak models, and is worse for the strong one

Cabinet chairman picking from the slate, restricted to questions where the correct answer is actually present to be recognized:

| Chairman | Can GENERATE it (solo) | Can RECOGNIZE it (pick from slate) | Gap |
|----------|------------------------|-------------------------------------|-----|
| Opus 4.8 (weak) | 49.5% | 65.0% | **+15.5 points** (n=103) |
| Fable 5 (strong) | 82.7% | 74.0% | **-8.7 points** (n=104) |

Peer-review ranking is a purer recognition test: each member ranks the four anonymized answers. Split by whether that member generated correctly, asking "did it rank a councilor holding the correct answer first?":

| Model | Recognition when it generated RIGHT | Recognition when it generated WRONG |
|-------|-------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| GPT-5.6 | 89% (39/44) | 50% (30/60) |
| Gemini | 92% (45/49) | 38% (20/52) |
| Grok | 92% (33/36) | 37% (25/68) |
| Fable | 87% (75/86) | **22% (4/18)** |

### Analysis

This is the mechanism behind Experiments 1 and 2.

- **For weak models, recognition clearly exceeds generation.** Opus picks correct answers it could not generate (+15.5). The three non-Fable members, on questions they got wrong, still recognize a peer's correct answer 37-50% of the time (against a generation rate of 0% on those questions by definition). For these models, Karpathy's ranking stage is doing real work.
- **For the strongest model, recognition is worse than generation.** Fable produces better answers than it can pick from a slate polluted by three wrong peers (-8.7). Worse, when Fable is wrong it recognizes the correct peer answer only **22%** of the time: it is confidently wrong and cannot be moved off it. All models recognize their own correct answers easily (87-92%); the discriminating cell is recognition-when-wrong, and there the strongest model is the weakest.

This resolves the design question directly. **The value of a cross-review / ranking stage is entirely regime-dependent.** In a council of near-equals every member has a positive recognition-minus-generation gap, so ranking helps (this is phase 1's result). In a council with one dominant member, that member's recognition is below its own generation and it cannot recognize its own errors, so ranking is redundant-to-harmful (this is phase 2's result). The recognition gap, not the structure, is the lever.

## Experiment 4: Where do councils help? (query-type sweep)

### Setup

No new calls. Slice every result by HLE category and ask two things per category: how often does the council disagree, and when it is unanimous, is it right?

### Result

Clean questions only:

| Category | n | Disagree rate | Unanimity accuracy | Fable solo | jury | cabinet | court |
|----------|---|---------------|--------------------|-----------|------|---------|-------|
| Humanities/Soc Sci | 75 | 56% | 76% | 77% | 48% | 71% | 67% |
| Math | 65 | 31% | 84% | 75% | 35% | 60% (n=20) | 55% |
| CS/AI | 56 | 48% | 79% | 75% | 37% | 59% | 63% |
| Other | 37 | 62% | 50% | 51% | 35% | 39% | 57% |
| Physics | 30 | 43% | 47% (n=17) | 50% | 8% | 46% | 38% |
| Engineering | 21 | 24% | 12% (n=16) | 29% | 80% (n=5) | 80% | 80% |

### Analysis

**The council's one reusable product is a difficulty signal, and it has a sharp exception.** Overall, unanimity predicts correctness: agree and you are right 67% of the time, disagree and accuracy collapses for every mechanism. Unanimity-as-accept, disagreement-as-escalate is a routing rule you get for free from independent sampling, with no deliberation.

But it breaks in the quantitative categories. **In Physics (unanimity 47%, n=17) and especially Engineering (unanimity 12%, n=16), a unanimous council is a warning sign, not a green light.** In Engineering the signal inverts: unanimous consensus is almost always wrong, while on the few disagreement cells every method hits 80% (n=5). Confident shared error concentrates exactly where plausible-but-wrong quantitative reasoning is easiest to agree on. Any router built on the unanimity signal needs a per-category override for these.

**Math is the only category where any structure edged solo Fable** (cabinet 60% vs 55%), and that is 12 vs 11 correct on n=20, one question of separation. It is a lead, not a result.

## Synthesis: are councils useful?

Not as answer-machines, in this regime. Yes as instruments, and in a different regime.

- **If you have the best model, do not convene a council to answer.** Every structure lost to solo Fable, and even putting Fable in the chair made it worse. You would pay several times the API cost to lose accuracy.
- **If your decider is mid-tier, a council lifts it.** Weak Opus gained 10 points as chairman, because recognition beats generation for weak models. A diverse council with your model deciding is a real accuracy tool when you cannot deploy the frontier model directly.
- **Use the council as a tripwire regardless.** Unanimity is a free confidence meter (67% correct), disagreement flags hard questions for escalation, with a mandatory per-category override in Physics and Engineering where the signal inverts.
- **The lever is the recognition gap, not the structure.** Councils help exactly when members can recognize better answers than they generate, which happens among near-equals and fails under one dominant member. This reconciles phase 1 (councils helped, near-equal small models) with phase 2 (councils hurt, one dominant frontier model): same theory, opposite regimes.

The one-line rule: **run the council to detect difficulty and to lift a weak model, never to override a strong one.**

## What this does not show

- **One benchmark family.** Everything here is verifiable multiple choice with a single right letter. Open-ended judgment, synthesis, and policy compliance are untested; phase 1 already showed MCQ results do not automatically transfer.
- **One regime.** Council v2 has a single dominant member. "Aggregation hurts" is a regime statement; phase 1's near-equals regime showed the opposite. Do not quote it without the qualifier.
- **The pool is tilted toward the dominant member.** Bio/chem were excluded because Fable refuses there. Pre-registered and necessary (Fable chairs), but it means Fable's 60.4% and 21/35 lone-correct share are measured on a pool curated around its refusal behavior.
- **Clean-pool attrition is not random.** 284 of 374 questions are clean; the drops are mostly Gemini's 70 no_answers and Fable's 13 refusals + 21 no_answers, which could bias the composition of the disagreement subset.
- **n = 130, single run, no seed replicates.** Endpoints of the structure ranking are real; the middle ordering is not (see Experiment 1). Category cells are small (Engineering unanimity n=16, Math edge n=20, Physics unanimity n=17): leads, not results.
- **Recognition uses arguments in the cabinet cut.** The cabinet chair sees reasoning excerpts, so its "recognition" is recognition-with-arguments; peer-review ranking is closer to pure recognition. Both point the same way, but the clean per-member decomposition waits on the arms.

## What's next: three questions this round did not answer

Three mechanism questions remain open. The first is answered in part by Experiment 3; the other two are untested and need new arms.

1. **Is recognition easier than generation? (partly answered.)** Experiment 3 says yes for weak models, no for the strongest. The clean per-member confirmation is arms A/B (`run_phase2_arms.py`, built and smoke-tested): Arm A shows each member the peers' anonymized answers with reasoning and allows revision (a wrong-to-right flip is recognition rescuing failed generation); Arm B is identical but positions-only, so A-vs-B separates persuasion-by-argument from herding-by-vote-count.

2. **Does identity cause deference? (not tested.)** Karpathy anonymizes answers before ranking on the assumption that models defer to high-status names. We followed the convention rather than testing it; every arm this round was anonymized, so we cannot infer it. A clean test is a de-anonymized arm: run one revision pass with real vendor labels versus anonymized, and measure whether members flip toward status-labeled answers more than toward the identical answer unlabeled. One evening's compute.

3. **Does more deliberation keep helping or collapse into agreement? (not tested.)** Everything this round is single-round: independent answers, then one aggregation pass. Whether a second round of discussion adds accuracy or just entrenches an early consensus is untested. The extension is an outer loop on Arm A: revise seeing peers, then show the new distribution and revise again, tracking accuracy and answer-variance per round. The one-round data already hints convergence is not free (cabinet made 16 rescues but 14 capitulations), so the live question is whether round two entrenches.

Beyond these: Rohit's compliance cases extend the updating-vs-herding measurement past verifiable MCQ into policy judgment where "correct" is defined by a spec; and the harness direction operationalizes Experiment 4's signal as a router (accept unanimity, escalate disagreement, override in Physics/Engineering).

