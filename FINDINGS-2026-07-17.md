# Findings: When Does a Frontier Council Beat Its Best Member?

2026-07-22. For Andy Hall and Rohit Krishnan. Repo `pairie-koh/llm-council-governance`, branch `main`. Every number reproduces offline from committed JSON with the commands in the appendix.

**Scope.** This study examines a council of **near-equal frontier models** — no single member dominates the field. That scope is deliberate: it is the regime in which aggregation has a chance to help, and it isolates the mechanism cleanly. Claims below are about near-equal councils; behaviour when one member is far stronger than the rest is out of scope here.

---

## The council and the setup

**Council:** the four models below, chosen so no one dominates (a spread of ~5 points, not a runaway leader). Opus 4.8 chairs/judges where a chair is needed.

| Model | Solo accuracy (clean set, n=295) |
|-------|----------------------------------|
| Gemini 3.1 Pro | 53.6% |
| GPT-5.6 Sol | 50.8% |
| Claude Opus 4.8 | 50.8% |
| Grok 4.5 | 48.5% |

**Benchmark:** Humanity's Last Exam, text-only multiple choice (Biology/Medicine and Chemistry pre-excluded for a chair-refusal reason inherited from the harness; 374 eligible questions). Each model answers once, independently. Of the questions where all four produce a scoreable answer, **295 are clean**, splitting into **155 unanimous** and **140 disagreement**. The 140 disagreement questions are the working set — unanimous questions are decided identically by every aggregation rule, so only disagreement discriminates. The baseline every council must beat is the **best single member on that subset: Gemini at 47.1%**.

**Paired design.** Every council structure re-aggregates the same frozen independent answers, so all comparisons are within-question: same questions, same member answers, only the aggregation rule changes.

---

## Finding 1: the premise of aggregation fails — errors are correlated

Wisdom-of-crowds (the Condorcet argument) needs the members' errors to be *independent*. They are not. Across the full pool, every pair of models is positively correlated, and when two are both wrong they pick the **same** wrong answer three-quarters of the time or more:

| Pair | phi | Same wrong answer (both wrong) |
|------|-----|--------------------------------|
| Gemini × Grok | +0.63 | 93/121 (77%) |
| Gemini × Opus | +0.63 | 93/114 (82%) |
| Grok × Opus | +0.62 | 120/146 (82%) |
| GPT × Gemini | +0.60 | 88/115 (77%) |
| GPT × Grok | +0.56 | 108/143 (76%) |
| GPT × Opus | +0.53 | 101/134 (75%) |

This is why plain voting cannot work: a majority does not average out independent noise, it concentrates shared error into a confident wrong answer. It sets up everything below — any structure that just counts votes is doomed, and the ones that win have to do something other than vote.

---

## Finding 2: selection structures beat the best member — voting and debate do not

Four ways to turn the four answers into one, scored on the 140 disagreement questions against the best member (Gemini, 47.1%):

| Structure | Mechanism | Accuracy | vs best member |
|-----------|-----------|----------|----------------|
| **cabinet** | a chair reads all four anonymized answers + reasoning and picks one | **52.9%** | **+5.8** |
| **peer review** | each member ranks the four; Borda count decides | **49.3%** | **+2.2** |
| court | one advocate argues each answer; a judge rules | 44.3% | −2.8 |
| jury | plain majority vote | 39.3% | −7.8 |

**The council beats its best member — but only through selection.** A chair directly picking the best answer (cabinet) wins by ~6 points (and by +2.7 at the pool level, 56.3% vs 53.6%); ranking (peer review) wins narrowly. **Adversarial debate (court) and plain voting (jury) both lose.** The jury is the worst by far: correlated errors turn into a confident wrong majority, so voting actively destroys accuracy.

The ordering tracks how directly the mechanism *selects* the best answer versus *processes* it. Direct selection wins; adding advocacy or reducing to a vote throws the advantage away.

*Statistical note (exact McNemar, n=140):* jury vs best member is decisive; cabinet's and peer review's wins are directional at this sample size. The endpoints — voting is catastrophic, selection helps — are solid; the exact cabinet-vs-peer gap is not.

---

## Finding 3: why selection works — recognition beats generation

*Generation* = producing the right answer alone. *Recognition* = picking the right answer out of a set of candidates. Selection-based aggregation only helps if recognition beats generation — and it does, across the council.

Measured directly on the chair: when Opus picks from the slate (on questions where the correct answer is present), it recognizes **66.7%** versus generating **52.3%** on its own — a **+14.4** point lift. It can pick right answers it could not produce.

The same holds per member. When a member answered *wrong* on its own, how often can it still rank a peer's correct answer first?

| Member | Recognizes a correct answer when it generated wrong |
|--------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| GPT-5.6 | 49% |
| Opus 4.8 | 43% |
| Grok 4.5 | 35% |
| Gemini 3.1 Pro | 30% |

Every member recovers a substantial share of answers it could not generate — that recovered signal is exactly what the cabinet harvests. **This is the engine of Finding 2:** the members collectively know more than any one can produce, and a selector can surface it. Note the twist — **the best member (Gemini) is the *worst* at recognizing a correct answer when it is itself wrong** (30%). The strongest generator is the most stubborn recognizer, a point Finding 4 sharpens.

## Finding 4: the mechanism is corrective conformity — and the best member is the least movable

Members re-answer after seeing their peers, in three conditions, to isolate *why* they update.

**It's conformity, not argument — and it's corrective.** Showing a member the peers' full *reasoning* (53.2% avg after) barely beats showing only their *votes* (51.5%): a ~1.7-point gap. So the ~8–10 point lift from seeing peers comes mostly from conforming to the distribution of answers, not from being argued into it. And the flips are ~2.5× more corrections (wrong→right) than capitulations (right→wrong). Among near-equals, the consensus carries real signal, so conforming to it is usually right.

**The best member is the least willing to hold a correct answer.** Hit with an evidence-free "you're wrong, reconsider" challenge, **Gemini — the best member — caved, dropping 47.1% → 38.6%**: it abandoned correct answers under baseless pressure. The weaker Opus did the opposite, using the prompt to reconsider productively (43.5% → 52.7%). The strongest model is the most suggestible about its own correct answers — consistent with it being the worst self-recognizer in Finding 3.

**"Council as armor" is not supported.** Does seeing peer support help a member resist a bad challenge? The effect is mixed and weak — peers helped GPT resist (+5.8pp) but made Grok cave *more* (−13.0pp), others near zero. No reliable protective effect. A clean negative result.

## Finding 5: the council's agreement is a difficulty signal — except in the quantitative fields

Even when it can't out-answer the best member, the council's *agreement* is information. When all four agree, they are right **59.4%** of the time; when they split, accuracy collapses. Unanimity-as-confidence is a free routing signal from independent sampling.

But it **inverts in the quantitative categories**:

| Category | Unanimity accuracy | 
|----------|--------------------|
| Math | 79% |
| Computer Science / AI | 72% |
| Humanities / Social Science | 63% |
| Physics | 44% (n=18) |
| Other | 43% (n=14) |
| **Engineering** | **0% (n=14)** |

In Physics and especially Engineering, a *unanimous* council is a warning sign, not a green light — in Engineering, unanimous consensus was wrong every time (0/14). Confident shared error concentrates exactly where plausible-but-wrong quantitative reasoning is easiest to agree on. Any router built on the agreement signal needs a per-category override there.

## Finding 6: verifiable coding — benchmark saturated (open)

The intended second query type was coding, to ask whether the ability to *run the tests* (objective verification) lets a council win where judgment could not. We built the full pipeline — HumanEval loader, subprocess execution sandbox, and a coding council with a verify switch. But on a stage-1 run, **all four models scored 100% pass@1** — zero disagreement, nothing for the council to select between. **HumanEval is saturated for 2026 frontier models.** The code is correct; the benchmark is solved. The verifiable query type remains open and needs a harder benchmark (LiveCodeBench or SWE-bench). Cost of establishing this: $0.99.

## Synthesis: councils are instruments, and selection is the instrument

Among near-equal frontier models:

- **A council beats its best member — through selection, not voting or debate.** A chair (or ranking) that picks the best of the members' answers wins by ~2–6 points; majority vote and adversarial court lose. If you convene a council, its job is to *select*, not to *deliberate to consensus*.
- **The reason is recognition beats generation.** The members collectively hold more correct answers than any one can produce; a selector surfaces them. That's the whole value.
- **Deliberation works by corrective conformity** — members conform to the consensus, and among near-equals the consensus is usually right. Arguments add little over the vote distribution.
- **The best member is the weak point of a council** — worst at recognizing its own errors, most likely to cave to baseless pressure. Do not let it dominate the aggregation.
- **The agreement signal is a free difficulty detector**, with a mandatory override in Physics and Engineering where confident consensus is confidently wrong.

The one-line rule: **use a council to select and to detect difficulty — not to vote, debate, or override a member's own reasoning.**

## What this does not show

- **Near-equal scope only.** These claims are for a council with no dominant member. A very strong member changes the picture (out of scope here by design).
- **One benchmark family.** Verifiable single-answer MCQ. Open-ended judgment (where "correct" is a spec, not a letter) is untested and is the natural next study.
- **The pool inherits a category exclusion** (Bio/Medicine and Chemistry) from the harness, so it is not the full HLE distribution.
- **n = 140, single run, no seed replicates.** The endpoints of the structure ranking (voting bad, selection good) are solid; the middle ordering is not. Category cells are small (Engineering, Physics, Other are n≤18) — leads, not results.
- **Coding is unanswered** (Finding 6) — the verifiable query type needs a harder benchmark.

---

## What's next

1. **Unverifiable judgment** — extend past MCQ into policy/compliance questions where "correct" is defined by a spec (Rohit's cases). The one place selection-vs-voting might behave differently. Blocked on data, not code.
2. **A harder coding benchmark** — LiveCodeBench/SWE-bench to answer whether verification lets a council win.
3. **Multi-round deliberation and identity/deference** — untested extensions of the arms.
4. **Applied:** operationalize the agreement signal as a router (accept unanimity, escalate disagreement, override Physics/Engineering) — including the prediction-market split where a council belongs on *resolvability validation*, not generative market design.

```

python -m experiments.run_council_types --analyze-only --council "$COUNCIL" \
  --output-dir experiments/results_council_fablefree      # Findings 1-2, 5
python -m experiments.run_phase2_arms --analyze-only \
  --output-dir experiments/results_arms_fablefree         # Finding 4
python -m experiments.run_coding_stage1 --analyze-only    # Finding 6
```

Finding 3's recognition figures are computed from `stage1_results.json` + `results_council_fablefree/council_types_results.json`.
